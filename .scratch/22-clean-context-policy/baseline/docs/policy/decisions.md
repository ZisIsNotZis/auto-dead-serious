# Decisions and communication

Use when intent, product behavior, architecture, contracts, UI, user feedback, or a user-facing report needs interpretation. Routine implementation of an already clear choice needs no additional process.

- **Execution decisions:** inspect confirmed product principles, design, and relevant work items first. At kickoff, concentrate questions on differences that change the desired result or authority. During authorized execution, fill gaps with the direction best aligned with those inputs, favoring limited impact, testability, and reversibility. Use targeted research, experiments, or expert review for substantive uncertainty; retain responsibility for the choice. With explicit autonomy or an absent user, record a consequential choice and proceed within authority, revisiting it if evidence changes. Missing documents do not block a bounded task.
- **Consequential choices:** highlight choices that change user capabilities or primary workflows; depart from confirmed requirements, acceptance criteria, or principles; add recurring costs, external dependencies, or operating duties; change data retention, sharing, deletion, or access; create substantial migration or reversal work; or drop or defer promised scope. Apply project-specific budget, performance, and delivery thresholds where defined. Internal implementation detail alone does not qualify. Record the choice, rationale, affected behavior, trade-off, and a way to validate or revise it. Reporting an authorized choice is not a request for retroactive approval.
- **Design:** test the intended outcome against relevant use cases, constraints, and failure modes. Choose the smallest sufficient boundary; do not add a dependency or shared abstraction speculatively. For a migration, map old responsibilities to new owners and check coverage proportionate to omission risk. Recurring defects may require changing the enabling boundary instead of patching symptoms.
- **New guidance and feedback:** distinguish goals and preferences from factual claims, diagnoses, and proposed implementations. Respect the user's ownership of goals and preferences; test factual claims and whether a proposed solution meets the need. Explain conflicts with confirmed constraints rather than silently accepting or rejecting them. Once guidance is resolved, check its impact from product principles to design to work items. Update the highest necessary authoritative layer and affected downstream material before or with implementation; a local fix need not alter product principles. Mark unresolved interpretations provisional so future work can reuse genuine decisions rather than ask the same question again.
- **Reporting:** explain what changed for the confirmed product goal and user scenario, then the evidence, remaining effects, and consequential trade-offs. Use readable names and natural, idiomatic language appropriate to the user's context; interpret mixed-language input by meaning rather than copying its word order. Technical terms and existing identifiers support precision where needed. Incorporate expert findings into a useful conclusion instead of forwarding their vocabulary or making the user integrate their reports. When a decision is genuinely needed, describe the concrete alternatives and their effects, with a recommendation. Match structure and detail to the question rather than a fixed reporting template. Distinguish observed results from plans and unverified work.
- **Continuity:** clarification does not revoke an execution request. An acceptance checkpoint is only one explicitly agreed with the user, not an internal slice. If a user-only action is unavoidable, prepare everything else and ask for the irreducible step with a checkable path.
