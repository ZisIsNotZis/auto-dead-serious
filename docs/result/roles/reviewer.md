# Reviewer: the cases it meets, and whether the documents answer them

What I judged: whether the documents tell the **reviewer** role what to do in every case it will meet. Not style, not correctness of the law, and I repaired nothing.

What I read, and nothing else: `agent-policy/AGENTS.md` (347 lines, the whole law including the Node guide) and `docs/method/review.md` (30 lines, the metric). I also had to establish where the reviewer's own guide is, so I read its frontmatter and its last 20 lines only — `agent-policy/agents/reviewer.md` lines 1-11 and 360-380 — and confirmed that lines 12-359 there are the same law as `AGENTS.md`. No other role's document, no project directory.

The reviewer's role-specific instruction is 20 lines, 180 words, appended to 347 lines of law written for a node that splits. That ratio is the cause of most of what follows.

## How each row was scored

The four verdicts are the metric's (`docs/method/review.md` lines 13-16): stated, delegated, ambiguous, silent. The reviewer's own three verdicts (pass / fail / blocked) are what the role emits; they are a different vocabulary and I did not confuse them.

`authority?` marks the cases the metric says may not be ambiguous or silent (review.md line 20: "A case about **who decides** may not be `ambiguous` or `silent`"). I marked a case **A** when the missing answer changes who may act: start, refuse the intake, write, release spend, raise a question, whose judgement stands, or what an approval licenses. I marked **A (shaded)** where the answer decides whether the reviewer may stop or refuse but the central question is evidence handling; those five are excluded from the count and reported separately.

## What the pattern is

The reviewer's document is a discipline for judging and not a session. It says what to weigh (acceptance, scope, revision binding), what to hunt for, and what the three verdicts mean — and it never says how a review starts, what the reviewer is, where the verdict lands, what may be spent, or how anything gets raised. Four of the five things it names as intake are contract-shaped, and the guide never makes the reviewer a party to that contract.

The verdict vocabulary is the sharper problem. The guide's three values cannot carry the three cases the same guide orders me to look for: the acceptance being the wrong acceptance (8), the check that could not run (10), and the question only a person can settle (13). So the more the reviewer does what its guide asks, the more often it must say something its own output format cannot hold — and the nearest available encoding is `blocked`, which the guide itself defines as an infrastructure fact and therefore as nobody's question.

What is stated is stated well: sibling disagreement (14), the clean pass (22), and the length rule for a reproduction (11) are all settled in one sentence each. Seven of the ten authority cases fail not because the law is silent but because the law's answer is addressed to a node with a roadmap, and the reviewer is told it is one by a paragraph, not given one by a directory.

## The count

**Ten of 22 cases score `ambiguous` or `silent` on a question of authority** — rows 1, 2, 3, 8, 13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21. By the metric (`docs/method/review.md` line 20) each of these is a defect that may not stand.

Five further cases — rows 5, 7, 10, 12, 20 — shade into authority, in that the missing answer decides whether the reviewer may refuse or stop; counted, the total is **15 of 22**. I have kept them out of the headline figure because their centre is evidence handling rather than decision rights.

Full distribution: stated 2, delegated 1, ambiguous 10, silent 9.

By the metric's self-consistency test, the reviewer's document commits both failures it names: it delegates freely ("Spend your judgement on", "Actively look for") without bounding the delegation, and it leaves authority open, which is the rule about consent broken in the document that is supposed to enforce consent.

## What I did not judge

Style, length, the correctness of the law's own provisions, the quality of the metric, and whether any project directory conforms to any of this. I also did not judge the other five role documents, by the metric's rule that a reviewer reads only its own.
