# Review

What one reviewer per role checks, and how it scores what it finds.

## The shape

A reviewer takes one role, reads only what that role actually receives, and enumerates the cases it will meet in the order a session meets them. For each case it asks one question: do the documents say what this role does next?

Each reviewer is independent. It reads the law and its own guide, and it does not read another role's guide, because a role that knows what the others were told will invent the answer instead of finding it missing.

## Four verdicts

- `stated`: the documents say what to do, and the answer can be checked after the fact.
- `delegated`: the documents say the choice belongs to this node, and say what bounds the choice.
- `ambiguous`: two readings are possible and they lead to different actions.
- `silent`: nothing addresses the case.

## What scores as a defect

A case about **who decides** may not be `ambiguous` or `silent`. Authority is the one thing a node cannot infer, and a node that infers it will infer in its own favour.

A case about **how to proceed** may be `delegated`, because how a node splits and works is its own business. An implicit delegation scores as `silent`: freedom nobody granted is freedom nobody can review.

## What the reviewer reports

One row per case, in the order it would be met: the case, the verdict, the sentence that settles it or the sentence that was missing, and what the role would most likely do instead when the answer is missing. A guess at the likely wrong action is worth more than a list of absences, because it is the thing that would actually happen.

## Self-consistency

This metric is the law applied to the law. A document that delegates a decision without saying so has broken the rule it states about reports, and a document that leaves authority open has broken the rule it states about consent.
